# Canto I
Psychology: How we judge and decide
Persuasion: The classic levers
Negotiation: The basics

## Day I

### Psychology | System 1 and System 2
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Your mind runs at two speeds: a fast, automatic one and a slow, effortful one.
**Explained:** Psychologist Daniel Kahneman made the labels famous in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). System 1 reads faces, finishes "bread and…" and jumps to answers without trying. System 2 does long sums and checks arguments, but it's lazy and tires easily. They're a way of talking, not two real parts of the brain.
**Example:** "A bat and a ball cost $1.10. The bat costs $1 more than the ball. How much is the ball?" Most people's first answer is 10 cents. That was System 1. System 2 finds 5 cents: the bat is $1.05, and together they make $1.10.
**Why it matters:** Most persuasion aims at System 1: urgency, images, round numbers. When a decision matters, slow down long enough for System 2 to check the work.

### Psychology | Heuristics
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Heuristics are mental shortcuts: rules of thumb that give a good-enough answer fast.
**Explained:** Economist Herbert Simon argued in the 1950s that we "satisfice" rather than weigh every option. In the 1970s, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed how shortcuts lead to predictable mistakes. Shortcuts aren't stupid; most of the time they work. Trouble comes when a situation only looks familiar, or when someone sets it up to trigger the shortcut.
**Example:** Picking the busy restaurant over the empty one is a heuristic: crowds usually mean good food. It fails when the crowd is one tour bus, and it's exploited when a club keeps a queue outside on purpose.
**Why it matters:** Nearly every trick in this app works by triggering a shortcut. Learn the shortcuts and you start to see the triggers.

### Psychology | Framing effect
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** The same facts, described differently, lead people to different choices.
**Explained:** In Tversky and Kahneman's 1981 study, most people picked a plan that would "save 200 of 600 people", but rejected the identical plan described as "400 people will die". A gain frame makes us careful; a loss frame makes us willing to gamble. The effect has been repeated many times.
**Example:** "90% fat-free" sells better than "10% fat". In one study, more people chose surgery when told "90% survive" than when told "10% die", and that included doctors.
**Why it matters:** Whenever you hear a number, flip it into the opposite frame. If your feeling changes but the facts didn't, the frame was doing the work.

### Persuasion | Ethos
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Ethos is persuasion through the speaker's character: we believe people we trust.
**Explained:** Aristotle's *Rhetoric* names three ways to persuade: ethos (character), pathos (emotion) and logos (reasoning). He said character may be the most powerful of the three. Trust, in his account, comes from good sense, good character and goodwill toward the audience. You can have the facts right and still lose if nobody trusts you.
**Example:** A doctor in a white coat, a founder who "started in a garage", a speaker who admits a small weakness first: each is building ethos before the argument even begins.
**Why it matters:** Ask who is speaking and why you trust them. Then ask whether that trust was earned on this topic, or borrowed from somewhere else.

### Persuasion | Pathos
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Pathos is persuasion through emotion: moving people to feel, not just to think.
**Explained:** Aristotle treated emotion as a fair part of persuasion, because we judge differently when we're angry, afraid or hopeful. Emotion isn't the opposite of reason; it tells us what we care about. The danger comes when feeling replaces thinking instead of starting it.
**Example:** Charity appeals show one named child rather than figures about millions, because a single face moves people more. Psychologists call this the identifiable victim effect.
**Why it matters:** When you notice a strong feeling, name it ("this is making me scared") and ask what decision it's pushing you toward.

### Negotiation | Positions vs interests
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** A position is what someone says they want; an interest is why they want it.
**Explained:** Roger Fisher and William Ury made this the heart of *Getting to Yes* (1981). Arguing over positions turns a deal into a tug of war. Asking about interests often reveals solutions neither side had thought of, because the same need can be met in more than one way.
**Example:** Two people in a library argue over a window: one wants it open, the other shut. The librarian asks why. One wants fresh air, the other wants no draught. She opens a window in the next room. Both get what they need.
**Why it matters:** Before you haggle, ask "what would that give you?" The answer is where the real deal is.

### Negotiation | Distributive vs integrative negotiation
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Distributive negotiation splits a fixed pie; integrative negotiation makes the pie bigger first.
**Explained:** In a distributive deal, one side's gain is the other's loss, like haggling over a used car's price. In an integrative deal there are several issues that each side values differently, so trading them leaves both better off. Most real negotiations are a mix, but people tend to treat them as pure haggling.
**Example:** Selling a sofa: the price is distributive. But you're moving out on Friday and need it gone, and the buyer has a van today. "Take it today for a little less" can suit you both.
**Why it matters:** If you only talk about price, you're only fighting. Put more issues on the table and look for trades.

## Day II

### Psychology | Loss aversion
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Losing something tends to feel worse than gaining the same thing feels good.
**Explained:** Kahneman and Tversky built it into prospect theory (1979), and it's often summed up as "losses hurt about twice as much". Later research is more mixed: the effect shows up clearly with bigger stakes and things we already own, but can be small or missing for everyday amounts. Real, but not a fixed law.
**Example:** Shops say "Don't miss out" and "Your basket is about to expire" rather than "Buy now". Free trials work partly because cancelling feels like losing something that's already yours.
**Why it matters:** When an offer is framed as avoiding a loss, ask what you'd actually give up, and whether you wanted it before someone told you it was yours.

### Psychology | Endowment effect
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We value things more just because they're ours.
**Explained:** In a well-known 1990 study by Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler, students given a university mug asked more than twice as much to sell it as other students would pay to buy one. Owning something seems to make it part of us. The effect is strongest for things we use, weaker for things bought to resell.
**Example:** People list their old sofa online for far more than similar sofas sell for, then wonder why nobody replies. The buyers aren't being cheap; the seller is attached.
**Why it matters:** In a negotiation, the other side's ownership inflates their price, and your ownership inflates yours. Check what similar things actually sell for.

### Psychology | Status quo bias
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We tend to stick with how things are, even when switching would be better.
**Explained:** Economists William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser named it in 1988. Changing feels like a risk we'd blame ourselves for; staying put feels like no decision at all. Loss aversion feeds it: what we give up by switching looms larger than what we'd gain.
**Example:** People keep the same phone plan, bank and energy supplier for years, often paying more than new customers, because switching takes effort and "it's fine".
**Why it matters:** Companies count on it with auto-renewals. Once a year, look at what you pay for out of habit and ask: would I choose this today?

### Persuasion | Logos
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Logos is persuasion through reasoning: evidence, logic and examples.
**Explained:** Aristotle's third mode appeals to the audience's reason. He noticed that everyday arguments rarely spell out every step: the speaker leaves a premise unsaid and lets the listener fill it in. That makes the listener part of the argument, which persuades, and it's also where weak reasoning hides.
**Example:** "This car came top in safety tests, so it's the family choice." The unsaid step is that safety scores matter most for your family. Maybe they do; maybe price or space matters more.
**Why it matters:** When an argument sounds airtight, look for the step nobody said out loud.

### Persuasion | Kairos
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Kairos is the right moment: the same message persuades at one time and fails at another.
**Explained:** The ancient Greeks had two words for time: *chronos*, clock time, and *kairos*, the opportune moment. Teachers of rhetoric stressed that timing, mood and occasion decide whether an argument lands. A good point made at the wrong moment is often a lost point.
**Example:** Asking for a raise the week after you landed a big client works better than asking during budget cuts. Gyms advertise hardest in January, while resolutions are fresh.
**Why it matters:** Notice when offers arrive: late at night, right after bad news, at the till. People who want your yes often choose the moment on purpose.

### Negotiation | BATNA
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Your BATNA is your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement: what you'll do if this deal falls through.
**Explained:** Fisher and Ury coined the term in *Getting to Yes*. Your BATNA is your real source of power: the better your alternative, the less you need this particular deal. It isn't a wish; it's the concrete next step you would actually take.
**Example:** With a written job offer of $60,000 in hand, you can calmly turn down anything worse from another employer. Without one, every offer feels like the only offer.
**Why it matters:** Before you negotiate, write down your BATNA and try to improve it. Then think about theirs: what happens to them if you walk away?

### Negotiation | Reservation price
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Your reservation price is your walk-away point: the worst deal you would still accept.
**Explained:** It follows from your BATNA: if your alternative is worth $60,000, a deal below that makes you worse off, other things being equal. Decide it before you start, while you're calm. In the moment, fatigue, a friendly seller and wanting to "win" push people past their own limit.
**Example:** A buyer decides the most they'll pay for a used car is $9,000. Two hours into a pleasant chat at the dealership, $9,400 starts to feel reasonable. Having $9,000 written down makes it easier to stand up and leave.
**Why it matters:** Don't reveal your reservation price, and don't let the conversation quietly move it.

## Day III

### Psychology | Default effect
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** People tend to stick with whatever option is already chosen for them.
**Explained:** A default takes no effort, feels like a recommendation, and switching away from it feels like a choice you could regret. In 2003, Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared European countries: where organ donation was opt-out, consent rates were far higher than where people had to opt in.
**Example:** Pension schemes that enrol workers automatically, with the option to leave, end up with far more savers than schemes where workers must sign up themselves.
**Why it matters:** Every form, app and contract has defaults chosen by someone. Before you click "next", check what's already ticked, and who benefits from it.

### Psychology | Halo effect
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** One good trait makes us see a person or product as good in other ways too.
**Explained:** Psychologist Edward Thorndike named it in 1920, after finding that army officers who rated a soldier highly on one quality tended to rate him highly on nearly everything. Good looks are a classic halo: attractive people are often assumed to be kinder and smarter. It works in reverse too: one flaw colours everything.
**Example:** A brand with beautiful packaging seems higher quality. A confident, well-dressed speaker seems more expert, even on topics far outside their field.
**Why it matters:** When judging someone, split the traits apart. Are they actually good at this specific thing, or just good at something else?

### Psychology | Stanford Prison Experiment
**Evidence:** Legend
**In short:** The 1971 study said ordinary students turned cruel when made prison guards. The real story is shakier.
**Explained:** Philip Zimbardo's study was stopped after six days and became famous as proof that situations make people evil. Later recordings and interviews showed guards were encouraged to be tough, a prisoner's breakdown was partly acted, and it was never a controlled experiment. A 2002 BBC version saw no tyrannical guards.
**Example:** The study still appears in textbooks and films as a lesson about evil. Yet the most notorious guard later said he was playing a role, modelled on a prison film he'd seen.
**Why it matters:** Situations do shape behaviour. But when one dramatic study "proves" something about human nature, ask how it was run and whether anyone repeated it.

### Persuasion | Reciprocity
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** When someone gives us something, we feel we owe them something back.
**Explained:** Robert Cialdini put it first among his principles of influence in *Influence* (1984). The urge to repay is found in every known culture and helps societies cooperate. It works even when the gift was never asked for, and a small gift can win a bigger favour in return.
**Example:** In one study, restaurant tips rose when waiters left a mint with the bill, and rose much more when the waiter came back with a second mint "just for you". Free samples in supermarkets work the same way.
**Why it matters:** Notice unasked-for gifts that come before a request. You can accept the gift without accepting the obligation.

### Persuasion | Door-in-the-face
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Ask for something big first; after the no, the smaller request you really wanted seems reasonable.
**Explained:** Cialdini's team tested it in 1975. Students asked strangers to mentor young offenders for two hours a week for two years; almost everyone refused. Then they asked for a single two-hour zoo trip with them. About three times as many agreed as when the zoo trip was asked for straight away.
**Example:** A teenager asks for a new phone, then settles for "just the headphones". A charity asks for a monthly pledge, then a one-off $10. The retreat feels like a concession, and we feel we should concede back.
**Why it matters:** When a first request seems oddly large, expect the "smaller" one next, and judge it on its own.

### Negotiation | ZOPA
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** The ZOPA, or Zone Of Possible Agreement, is the overlap between what each side would accept.
**Explained:** If a seller's walk-away point is $8,000 and a buyer's is $10,000, any price in between works for both: that's the ZOPA. If the seller won't go below $11,000 and the buyer won't go above $10,000, there's no ZOPA and no deal, however skilful anyone is.
**Example:** A landlord needs at least $1,200 a month; you can pay up to $1,350. The deal will land somewhere in that $150 gap, and negotiating skill decides where.
**Why it matters:** You rarely know the other side's limit, so ask questions and watch reactions. And if there truly is no ZOPA, walking away isn't failure.

### Negotiation | First offers
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Whoever names the first number often pulls the final deal toward it.
**Explained:** A first offer works as an anchor. In a 2001 study, Adam Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler found the side that made the first offer usually got the better result. But it only helps if you know roughly what the thing is worth; if you don't, let them go first.
**Example:** A freelancer who opens with "My rate is $80 an hour" usually ends up higher than one who asks "What's your budget?" and hears $40.
**Why it matters:** If you know the value, open ambitiously, with a reason you can defend. If they open with something extreme, don't bargain from it: set it aside and restate your own number.

## Day IV

### Psychology | Fundamental attribution error
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We blame other people's actions on their character, but explain our own by the situation.
**Explained:** Psychologist Lee Ross named it in 1977. In an earlier study, people read essays praising Fidel Castro and judged the writers to be pro-Castro, even when told the writers had been assigned that side. The error is weaker in cultures that pay more attention to context, but it's reliable.
**Example:** Someone cuts you off in traffic: "What an idiot." You cut someone off: "I didn't see them, and I'm running late."
**Why it matters:** Before deciding what someone is like, ask what their situation might be. In a negotiation, the other side's "stubbornness" may be a boss who won't let them move.

### Psychology | Self-serving bias
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We take credit for our successes and blame outside factors for our failures.
**Explained:** It protects our self-esteem, and it's one of the most consistently found biases. Students credit a good grade to hard work and a bad one to an unfair exam. Companies do it too: annual reports tend to credit good years to management and bad years to the economy.
**Example:** In a 1979 study, married couples were each asked what share of the household tasks they did. Their two answers usually added up to well over 100%.
**Why it matters:** When judging your own results, ask what you'd say if a friend had the same outcome. And expect the other side to remember its own contribution as bigger than you do.

### Psychology | Hindsight bias
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Once we know how something turned out, we feel we "knew it all along".
**Explained:** Baruch Fischhoff showed it in the 1970s. People asked to predict events before President Nixon's 1972 trips to China and the USSR later remembered their predictions as closer to what actually happened. Knowing the ending rewrites our memory of how uncertain things felt.
**Example:** After a company collapses, the warning signs look obvious in every article. Before the collapse, the same signs were buried among thousands of others that meant nothing.
**Why it matters:** It makes us overconfident, and unfair to people who decided under uncertainty. Judge a decision by what could be known at the time, not by how it turned out.

### Persuasion | Commitment and consistency
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Once we've committed to something, we feel pressure to act consistently with it.
**Explained:** Cialdini found commitments stick best when they're active, public and feel freely chosen. Consistency is useful: people who keep their word are trusted. But it means a small early yes can quietly bind us to a bigger one we'd never have agreed to on its own.
**Example:** In a 1968 study, people who had just placed a bet at a racetrack were more confident their horse would win than people still waiting in the queue. Having committed, they believed in it more.
**Why it matters:** When you feel "I've already said yes, so I should", ask: knowing what I know now, would I say yes again?

### Persuasion | Foot-in-the-door
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Get a small yes first, and a bigger request becomes more likely to succeed.
**Explained:** In a 1966 study, Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser asked homeowners to put a large, ugly "Drive Carefully" sign in their front garden. About 17% agreed. Among those who had earlier agreed to a tiny "Be a safe driver" sticker, about 76% said yes. The first yes changed how people saw themselves.
**Example:** A website asks for your email "to save your progress", then your phone number, then a subscription. Each step feels small because you've already said yes.
**Why it matters:** Watch for a chain of small requests heading somewhere. You're allowed to stop partway.

### Negotiation | Target price
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Your target is the best realistic deal you're aiming for, set before you start.
**Explained:** It's different from your walk-away point. Research going back to Sidney Siegel and Lawrence Fouraker in 1960 finds that negotiators with specific, ambitious goals get better outcomes than those who just "do their best". Oddly, they can feel less satisfied, because the result fell short of the goal.
**Example:** Two friends sell identical bikes. One thinks "I'll see what I can get". The other checked recent sales and aims for $450. The second one usually gets more.
**Why it matters:** Set a target from research, aim for it, and don't let a low opening offer from the other side quietly lower it.

### Negotiation | Precise numbers
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** A precise number like $4,850 tends to anchor more strongly than a round one like $5,000.
**Explained:** A 2013 study by Malia Mason and colleagues found precise first offers pulled final deals closer to them: people assume a precise figure was carefully worked out, so they move less. Later studies mostly agree, though a number that seems precise for no reason can backfire.
**Example:** A house listed at $497,500 tends to draw smaller counter-offers than one at $500,000. A salary ask of $63,500 suggests research; $60,000 sounds like a guess.
**Why it matters:** When you name a number, make it precise and be ready to explain it. When you hear one, remember that precision isn't proof.

## Day V

### Psychology | Overconfidence
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We tend to think we know more, and are right more often, than we really are.
**Explained:** When people say they're "99% sure" of their answers to general-knowledge questions, they're wrong far more than 1% of the time. In a 1981 study, about 93% of American students rated themselves as better than average drivers. Overconfidence is strongest on hard questions.
**Example:** Many new businesses fail within a few years, yet in one 1988 survey, a third of new business owners said their own firm had no chance of failing at all.
**Why it matters:** When you feel certain, ask "how often am I this sure, and wrong?" In negotiation, overconfident people turn down good deals expecting better ones.

### Psychology | Planning fallacy
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We underestimate how long our own plans will take, even when similar plans ran late.
**Explained:** Kahneman and Tversky named it in 1979. We picture the plan going smoothly and forget the delays that usually crop up. Oddly, we're better at predicting other people's delays than our own.
**Example:** The Sydney Opera House was planned in 1957 to open in 1963 at a cost of about 7 million Australian dollars. It opened in 1973 and cost around 102 million.
**Why it matters:** Look at how long similar projects actually took, not at your plan. When someone promises a deadline in a deal, ask what happened last time.

### Psychology | Power posing
**Evidence:** Legend
**In short:** The claim that standing like a superhero for two minutes changes your hormones and your confidence.
**Explained:** A 2010 study by Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap reported that "power poses" raised testosterone, lowered stress hormones and increased risk-taking. A 2012 TED talk made it famous. Larger studies found no hormone effects, and in 2016 Carney said she no longer believed the effects were real.
**Example:** Millions watched the talk and tried power poses before interviews. Feeling a little bolder is plausible, and a small effect on feeling powerful may remain. Changing your body chemistry wasn't supported.
**Why it matters:** A great talk isn't the same as strong evidence. When a simple trick promises big changes, check whether anyone repeated the study.

### Persuasion | Low-ball technique
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Get agreement on an attractive deal, then change the terms; many people stick with it anyway.
**Explained:** Cialdini's team showed it in 1978: students agreed to take part in a study, then learned it started at 7 a.m. Far more stayed signed up than when the early start was mentioned first. Once we've decided, we find new reasons to stay committed, even when the original reason has gone.
**Example:** A car dealer agrees a great price, you fill in the forms, then the manager "can't approve it" and the price goes up a little. Many buyers pay anyway.
**Why it matters:** When the terms change after you've said yes, treat it as a brand-new decision. You're entitled to walk away.

### Persuasion | That's-not-all technique
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Adding a bonus before the buyer has answered makes a deal feel better than offering everything at once.
**Explained:** Psychologist Jerry Burger tested it in 1986 at a bake sale. Some buyers were told a cupcake with two cookies cost 75 cents. Others heard "cupcakes are 75 cents", then, before they could reply, "and that includes two cookies". The second group bought far more.
**Example:** "You get the blender. But wait, that's not all: we'll add a second one free!" TV shopping channels are built on it.
**Why it matters:** When extras keep appearing before you've even asked, judge what the whole package is worth, not how fast it seems to be growing.

### Negotiation | Concession patterns
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** How you give ground tells the other side how much more is left.
**Explained:** Concessions that shrink each time, say $500, then $200, then $50, signal you're close to your limit. Big or equal steps suggest there's plenty more room. Giving ground too quickly also makes the other side value what they get less.
**Example:** A seller who drops from $10,000 to $9,000 to $8,000 invites the buyer to expect $7,000 next. Moving from $10,000 to $9,400 to $9,200 to $9,150 says "almost done".
**Why it matters:** Plan your moves before you start, make each one smaller, and read the other side's pattern the same way.

### Negotiation | Reciprocal concessions
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** When one side gives ground, the other side feels pressure to give ground too.
**Explained:** It's reciprocity applied to bargaining, and it's what makes door-in-the-face work. Negotiations tend to move in steps: I move, you move. A concession that costs you little but matters to them can unlock a bigger one in return.
**Example:** "I can't lower the price, but I can include delivery. Could you pay this week instead of next month?" Each side gives something and gets something.
**Why it matters:** Don't give a concession for nothing: attach it to a request ("If I do this, will you do that?"). And notice when the other side's "concession" was never really on the table.

## Day VI

### Psychology | Asch conformity experiments
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** In the 1950s, many people gave an obviously wrong answer rather than disagree with a group.
**Explained:** Solomon Asch asked people which of three lines matched a reference line. The others in the room were actors who, on some rounds, all gave the same wrong answer. About a third of answers went along with the group, and three in four people conformed at least once. One ally who disagreed cut conformity sharply.
**Example:** In a meeting, everyone nods at a plan you think has a flaw. Saying so feels much harder than it should.
**Why it matters:** Groups pull us toward agreement. If you disagree, say so early: you may be the ally someone else is waiting for.

### Psychology | In-group bias
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We favour people we see as "our side", even when the sides mean almost nothing.
**Explained:** In Henri Tajfel's "minimal group" studies in the early 1970s, boys were told they'd been grouped by whether they preferred paintings by Klee or Kandinsky; the groups were really random. They still gave more rewards to their own group. It takes very little to create an "us".
**Example:** Strangers trust each other more when they wear the same team's shirt. Salespeople look for a shared hometown, school or hobby for a reason.
**Why it matters:** When someone quickly finds what you have in common, enjoy it, but notice that it's making you more trusting.

### Psychology | Bystander effect
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** The idea that people are less likely to help in an emergency when others are around.
**Explained:** In 1960s lab studies, John Darley and Bibb Latané found people reacted more slowly when they believed others were present. But the case that made it famous, 38 neighbours ignoring Kitty Genovese's murder in 1964, was badly exaggerated. A 2019 study of real CCTV footage found someone helped in about 9 of 10 public conflicts.
**Example:** When someone collapses in a busy street, the first few seconds can be full of people waiting for someone else to act. Once one person steps in, others usually follow.
**Why it matters:** If you need help in a crowd, pick one person: "You in the blue jacket, call an ambulance." Responsibility shared by everyone feels like no one's.

### Persuasion | Social proof
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** When we're unsure what to do, we look at what others are doing.
**Explained:** Cialdini listed it as a core principle: other people's behaviour is useful information, especially when things are uncertain and the others seem like us. It saves effort, but it can be faked, and it spreads mistakes as easily as good choices.
**Example:** "Best seller", "4.8 stars from 12,000 reviews", "23 people are looking at this hotel". In a 1969 study, the more people stood in a street staring up at nothing, the more passers-by stopped and looked up too.
**Why it matters:** Check whether the crowd is real, and whether it's a crowd like you. Popular isn't the same as right for you.

### Persuasion | Descriptive norms
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Telling people what most others do is powerful, which means it can backfire.
**Explained:** A descriptive norm is what people actually do; an injunctive norm is what they should do. Cialdini found that warnings like "many people do this bad thing" can spread the very behaviour they're meant to stop, because they make it sound normal.
**Example:** In Arizona's Petrified Forest National Park, a sign saying many visitors had taken wood led to more theft than a sign simply asking people not to take any.
**Why it matters:** When you want someone to do something, show that most people already do it. When you hear "everyone does it", ask whether that's true, or just a way to make it feel normal.

### Negotiation | Separate the people from the problem
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Deal with relationships and emotions separately from the issues being negotiated.
**Explained:** It's the first principle of *Getting to Yes*. Negotiators are people, with feelings, pride and their own view of the facts. When the relationship gets tangled up with the issue, every disagreement feels personal and people dig in to save face. Fisher and Ury's advice: be soft on the people, hard on the problem.
**Example:** Instead of "You're being unreasonable about the rent", try "We see the rent differently. Let's look at what similar flats cost." Now you're solving a problem together, not fighting each other.
**Why it matters:** Let the other side save face, acknowledge their feelings, and aim your pushback at the problem. People agree more readily when they don't feel they're losing.

### Negotiation | Objective criteria
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Settle disagreements by fair standards both sides can accept, not by who pushes harder.
**Explained:** Another principle from *Getting to Yes*: instead of a battle of wills, look for an outside measure, such as market prices, an expert valuation, past practice or what a court would decide. It's easier to accept a standard than to give in to the other person.
**Example:** Two neighbours argue over paying for a new fence. Instead of haggling, they get two quotes and split the lower one.
**Why it matters:** Ask "What's that based on?" and suggest a standard. Then check the standard itself is fair: a "market price" picked by one side isn't neutral.

## Day VII

### Psychology | Milgram obedience experiments
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** In the 1960s, many ordinary people gave what they thought were painful electric shocks because an authority told them to.
**Explained:** In Stanley Milgram's best-known version, 65% went all the way to the maximum 450 volts. The shocks were fake and the "learner" was an actor. Later research found many participants doubted the shocks were real, and obedience varied hugely between versions of the study.
**Example:** When the experimenter gave orders by phone instead of in the room, obedience fell to about 21%. When two other "participants" refused to continue, it fell to 10%.
**Why it matters:** Obedience is real, but so is the power of refusing. If an instruction feels wrong, saying no can make it easier for others to say no too.

### Psychology | Just-world belief
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We like to believe the world is fair, so people must deserve what happens to them.
**Explained:** Psychologist Melvin Lerner studied it from the 1960s. Believing the world is fair makes life feel safer and more controllable. But it leads people to blame victims: if something bad happened to someone, they must have done something to cause it.
**Example:** In a 1966 study by Lerner, people who watched someone apparently receive electric shocks, and couldn't help, tended to rate the victim less favourably afterwards.
**Why it matters:** When you hear "they must have brought it on themselves", ask whether that's known, or just comforting. Scammers rely on it too: "only foolish people get scammed" keeps victims quiet.

### Psychology | Negativity bias
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Bad things grab our attention and stick in memory more than equally good things.
**Explained:** A 2001 review by Roy Baumeister and colleagues, titled "Bad Is Stronger Than Good", found the pattern in relationships, feedback, first impressions and learning. It probably helped our ancestors survive: missing a threat costs more than missing an opportunity.
**Example:** Nine compliments and one criticism in a review, and it's the criticism you replay that night. Bad news headlines tend to draw more clicks than good ones.
**Why it matters:** Persuaders use it through fear and warnings. In feedback and negotiation, one harsh remark can outweigh a lot of goodwill, so choose criticisms carefully.

### Persuasion | Authority
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We tend to follow people who seem to be experts or in charge.
**Explained:** Cialdini's principle builds on Milgram: titles, uniforms and confident expertise make us more likely to comply. Following real experts is usually sensible. The problem is that the signs of authority are easy to fake, and we tend to respond to the signs rather than the expertise.
**Example:** In a 1966 hospital study, 21 of 22 nurses were ready to give an overdose of a drug when an unknown "doctor" ordered it by phone. A later study, using a drug the nurses knew, found far fewer went along.
**Why it matters:** Ask whether this person is an expert on *this* question, and whether they gain from your yes. A title or a confident voice isn't evidence.

### Persuasion | Unity
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We say yes more easily to people we feel are "one of us".
**Explained:** Cialdini added unity as a seventh principle in *Pre-Suasion* (2016). It goes deeper than liking: it's about shared identity, such as family, hometown, faith or team. When someone feels like part of "us", their interests start to feel like ours.
**Example:** A friend-of-a-friend asking for a favour gets far more help than a stranger. A sales pitch that opens "As a parent myself…" is reaching for unity.
**Why it matters:** Shared identity is a real bond, but it's easy to claim. Ask whether the "we" is real, or just a word someone started using.

### Negotiation | Expanding the pie
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Before splitting value, look for ways to create more of it.
**Explained:** This is the heart of integrative negotiation. Each side usually values things differently: timing, risk, extras, certainty. Adding issues or options can leave both better off. Fisher and Ury called it "inventing options for mutual gain", and advised brainstorming before deciding anything.
**Example:** A band wants a bigger fee than a small venue can afford. Instead they agree a lower fee plus a share of ticket sales above a certain number. The venue risks less; the band can earn more.
**Why it matters:** When you're stuck on one number, ask "What else could we trade?" The best deals often come from adding, not cutting.

### Negotiation | Logrolling
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Trading on issues you care about differently: giving way on what matters less to you for what matters more.
**Explained:** The term comes from neighbours helping each other roll logs, and later from politicians backing each other's bills. In negotiation, it's how integrative deals are built. It only works if each side knows its own priorities and learns something about the other side's.
**Example:** A new hire cares most about working from home; the company cares most about salary costs. Trading a slightly lower salary for two remote days a week can beat haggling over salary alone.
**Why it matters:** Rank your issues before you negotiate. Then trade the ones low on your list for the ones high on theirs.

## Day VIII

### Psychology | Mere exposure effect
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We tend to like things more simply because we've seen or heard them before.
**Explained:** Robert Zajonc showed it in 1968 with made-up words, Chinese characters and faces: the more often people saw them, the more they liked them. Familiarity feels safe. The effect is strongest for brief exposures, and it fades or reverses if something is repeated to the point of annoyance.
**Example:** A song you disliked at first becomes a favourite after hearing it on the radio a few times. Brands pay to show the same logo again and again, even when you barely notice it.
**Why it matters:** Liking something can simply mean you've seen it a lot. Ask whether you actually prefer it, or just know it.

### Psychology | Cognitive dissonance
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Holding conflicting beliefs, or acting against them, feels uncomfortable, so we change something to make it stop.
**Explained:** Leon Festinger developed the theory in 1957. In a famous 1959 study, people paid $1 to tell someone a boring task was fun later rated the task as more enjoyable than people paid $20. With little reason to lie, they changed their opinion instead.
**Example:** After buying an expensive phone, people seek out reviews that praise it and skip the ones that criticise it. The purchase is done, so the doubts have to go.
**Why it matters:** If you catch yourself defending a choice harder than it deserves, dissonance may be talking. Persuaders use it by getting you to act first; your beliefs then follow.

### Psychology | Ben Franklin effect
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** The idea that people like you more after doing you a favour.
**Explained:** Benjamin Franklin wrote that he won over a rival lawmaker by asking to borrow a rare book, and the man became a friend. A small 1969 study by Jon Jecker and David Landy found people liked a researcher more after doing him a favour. It fits dissonance theory, but the evidence is thin.
**Example:** A new colleague asks your advice on something you know well. Afterwards you feel warmer toward them, partly because you helped.
**Why it matters:** Asking for a small favour can build goodwill, but don't lean on it as a trick. And notice when someone's request is really a way to get you invested.

### Persuasion | Liking
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We say yes more easily to people we like.
**Explained:** Cialdini listed the main causes: good looks, similarity, compliments, familiarity and working together toward a shared goal. Liking and persuasion are tangled: we tend to believe people we like, and to like people who agree with us.
**Example:** Tupperware parties sell to the host's friends, not strangers. Buying feels like doing a friend a favour, and the friend, not the salesperson, does most of the persuading.
**Why it matters:** Separate the person from the offer: "Would I buy this from someone I didn't like?" If not, it's the friendship that's selling.

### Persuasion | Mirroring
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Subtly copying someone's posture, gestures or words can make them like and trust you more.
**Explained:** People naturally mimic those they like, and some studies suggest it works in reverse too. In a 2003 Dutch study, waitresses who repeated customers' orders back word for word got bigger tips. But results vary, and obvious copying tends to backfire when people notice.
**Example:** Former FBI negotiator Chris Voss recommends repeating the last few words someone said, as a question: "…the deadline is Friday?" People tend to keep talking and explain more.
**Why it matters:** Echoing someone's words shows you're listening. If someone is mirroring you closely, it may be rapport, or it may be technique.

### Negotiation | Fixed-pie bias
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** The assumption that whatever you gain, the other side must lose.
**Explained:** Research by Leigh Thompson and Reid Hastie in 1990 found most negotiators wrongly assume the other side's priorities are the exact opposite of their own. So they miss trades that would help both, and sometimes even fight over issues where they want the same thing.
**Example:** A buyer and seller both want a quick sale, but each assumes the other wants to wait, and neither says so. They haggle over a timing they already agree on.
**Why it matters:** Don't assume you know what the other side wants. Ask, and share some of your own priorities: you may agree on more than you think.

### Negotiation | Reactive devaluation
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We value a proposal less simply because it comes from the other side.
**Explained:** Lee Ross and colleagues studied it from the late 1980s. If the other side offers something, we suspect a catch, so the same proposal seems worse coming from them than from our own side, or from a neutral party.
**Example:** In a 2002 study, Israeli participants rated a real Israeli peace proposal less favourably when told it had come from the Palestinian side.
**Why it matters:** Judge an offer by what it contains, not by who made it. And if you want something accepted, it can help to let the other side suggest it.

## Day IX

### Psychology | Spotlight effect
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We overestimate how much other people notice us.
**Explained:** In a 2000 study by Thomas Gilovich and colleagues, students wore an embarrassing Barry Manilow T-shirt into a room of other students. They guessed about half the room would notice it. In fact, only about a quarter did. Everyone is busy being the centre of their own attention.
**Example:** You stumble over a word in a presentation and replay it all evening. Most of the audience didn't notice, and the few who did have already forgotten.
**Why it matters:** Fear of looking foolish stops people asking questions or making counter-offers. Others are paying far less attention to you than you think.

### Psychology | Illusion of transparency
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We think our feelings show more clearly than they really do.
**Explained:** Gilovich and colleagues found in 1998 that people who told lies, or felt nervous giving a speech, overestimated how easily others could tell. Our feelings are vivid to us, so we assume they're leaking out for everyone to see.
**Example:** A nervous speaker thinks everyone can see them shaking; the audience rates them as fairly calm. A negotiator thinks their eagerness to close is obvious; the other side hasn't noticed.
**Why it matters:** You're harder to read than you think, so don't rush to fill silences or give things away. And don't assume others know what you want: say it.

### Psychology | Curse of knowledge
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Once we know something, it's hard to imagine not knowing it.
**Explained:** In a 1990 Stanford study by Elizabeth Newton, people tapped out the rhythm of well-known songs and predicted listeners would name about half of them. Listeners named about 1 in 40. The tappers could hear the tune in their heads; the listeners heard only knocking.
**Example:** Experts explain things in jargon that feels obvious to them. Instructions written by the person who built the product skip steps they no longer notice.
**Why it matters:** When you explain or persuade, test your message on someone who knows nothing about it. And when someone says "it's simple", ask them to show you step by step.

### Persuasion | Scarcity
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Things seem more valuable when they're rare or might soon be gone.
**Explained:** Cialdini's principle mixes two ideas: rare things often are valuable, and losing the freedom to choose makes us want it more. In a 1975 study by Stephen Worchel and colleagues, people rated cookies from a jar holding two as more desirable than identical cookies from a jar holding ten.
**Example:** "Only 2 left in stock", "Offer ends at midnight", limited-edition trainers that sell out in minutes. Sometimes the scarcity is real; sometimes it's designed.
**Why it matters:** Ask whether you'd want it as much if there were plenty. If not, it's the scarcity you're responding to, not the thing.

### Persuasion | Contrast principle
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We judge things by comparison, so what comes first changes how the next thing looks.
**Explained:** Put one hand in cold water and one in hot, then both in lukewarm water: it feels warm to one hand and cool to the other. Cialdini showed persuaders do the same with prices and requests, showing something expensive or unpleasant first so the next thing looks better.
**Example:** An estate agent shows a run-down, overpriced house before the one they want to sell. A clothes shop sells the suit first; the $60 tie then seems cheap.
**Why it matters:** When something looks like a bargain, ask "compared with what?", and whether that comparison was put there on purpose.

### Negotiation | Deadlines
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Deadlines push both sides toward agreement, and most concessions happen near the end.
**Explained:** In most negotiations, the biggest moves come in the final stretch. A 2004 study by Don Moore found that negotiators who revealed their deadline often did better, because the other side realised it faced the same time pressure. Deadlines hurt most when only you know about yours, and believe the other side has all the time in the world.
**Example:** Strikes are often averted in the final hours before they're due to start. Both sides knew the deadline, and both moved at the last minute.
**Why it matters:** Don't let an artificial deadline rush you: ask whether it's real. And remember the other side almost always has time pressure too.

### Negotiation | Leverage
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Leverage is whatever makes the other side need the deal more than you do.
**Explained:** It comes from alternatives (a strong BATNA), from time (who can afford to wait), from information, and from what each side would lose if talks fail. Leverage isn't fixed: it shifts as options appear or disappear, and it depends partly on what each side believes.
**Example:** A tenant with a new job in another city, and other flats available, has leverage when the lease is up. A tenant who must stay for their children's school has less, unless the landlord doesn't know it.
**Why it matters:** Build leverage before you negotiate: find alternatives and learn what the other side stands to lose. And be careful what you reveal about your own pressures.

## Day X

### Psychology | Reactance
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** When we feel our freedom is being taken away, we push back, often by wanting the forbidden thing more.
**Explained:** Psychologist Jack Brehm described it in 1966. Being told we can't, or must, do something triggers an urge to restore our freedom. It explains why heavy-handed warnings backfire, and why banning a book can make it a best seller.
**Example:** In a 1976 study, a toilet wall sign saying "Do not write on these walls under any circumstances" drew more graffiti than a milder "Please don't write on these walls".
**Why it matters:** Pressure creates resistance, so skilled persuaders leave you the choice: "It's up to you, of course." When you feel a strong urge to do the opposite of what you're told, check whether you actually want it.

### Psychology | Ego depletion
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** The idea that willpower is a limited fuel that runs out as you use it.
**Explained:** Roy Baumeister's 1998 studies suggested that resisting temptation, like eating radishes instead of chocolate cookies, made people give up sooner on a later puzzle. Hundreds of studies followed. But a large replication across 23 labs in 2016 found an effect close to zero, and later tests found small effects at most.
**Example:** The idea spread into advice like "sugar restores your self-control". That claim didn't hold up either.
**Why it matters:** Tiredness really does affect decisions, but the neat "fuel tank" model is doubtful. Be wary of popular advice built on one famous study.

### Psychology | Marshmallow test
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Children who waited longer for a second treat did better in life, the story goes. The truth is more modest.
**Explained:** In Walter Mischel's Stanford studies from the late 1960s, children could eat one marshmallow now or wait for two. Follow-ups linked waiting with later exam scores, but the samples were small and mostly children of Stanford staff. A larger 2018 study found the link shrank by about two-thirds once family background was counted.
**Example:** Children also wait longer when they trust the adult: in a 2013 study, they waited far longer if the researcher had kept an earlier promise to them.
**Why it matters:** Self-control matters, but so does whether waiting makes sense. Before believing one test predicts a life, ask what else might explain the results.

### Persuasion | The "because" effect
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Giving a reason, even an empty one, can make people more likely to agree to a small request.
**Explained:** In a 1978 study led by Ellen Langer, people asked to skip the queue at a photocopier. "May I use the Xerox machine?" got 60% yes. Adding "because I'm in a rush" got 94%. The empty "because I have to make copies" still got 93%. For bigger requests, the empty reason stopped working.
**Example:** "Could you swap shifts with me, because I've got a thing?" often works, not because of the reason, but because a reason was given at all.
**Why it matters:** It's one small, old study, so hold it lightly. But for anything that matters, listen to the reason itself, not just the word "because".

### Persuasion | Illusory truth effect
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Hearing a claim again and again makes it seem more true, even when it isn't.
**Explained:** Researchers first showed it in 1977: people rated repeated statements as more likely to be true than new ones. Repetition makes a claim feel familiar and easy to process, and we mistake that ease for truth. A 2015 study found it works even when people already know the right answer.
**Example:** Myths like "we only use 10% of our brains" survive mainly because we've heard them so often. Slogans and soundbites rely on the same effect.
**Why it matters:** Familiarity isn't evidence. When something "just sounds right", ask where you actually learned it.

### Negotiation | Asking questions
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** In a negotiation, good questions usually get you more than good arguments.
**Explained:** In a 1970s study of skilled and average negotiators, Neil Rackham found the skilled ones asked more than twice as many questions. Questions uncover the other side's interests, priorities and limits, and they make the other side feel heard.
**Example:** Instead of arguing that a price is too high, ask "How did you arrive at that figure?" or "What matters most to you in this deal?" The answers often show where there's room.
**Why it matters:** Open questions ("what", "how") find information. Yes-or-no questions mostly confirm what you already think you know.

### Negotiation | Information asymmetry
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** When one side knows more than the other, the side with more information usually gets the better deal.
**Explained:** Economist George Akerlof showed in 1970, in "The Market for Lemons", how it can break a market: if buyers can't tell good used cars from bad ones ("lemons"), they won't pay much for any, so owners of good cars stop selling. He shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in economics for this work.
**Example:** The seller of a used car knows its history; the buyer doesn't. That's why warranties, inspections and vehicle history reports exist: they shrink the gap.
**Why it matters:** Before negotiating, close the gap: research prices, get inspections, ask for evidence. And ask what the other side knows that you don't.

## Day XI

### Psychology | Peak-end rule
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and how it ended, not by its total.
**Explained:** In a 1993 study by Kahneman and colleagues, people held a hand in painfully cold water for 60 seconds, and in another trial for those 60 seconds plus 30 more at a slightly warmer temperature. Most chose to repeat the longer trial, because it ended less badly.
**Example:** A holiday with one amazing day and a smooth trip home is remembered better than a steadily good one that ended with a lost suitcase. Good restaurants end with a small free treat.
**Why it matters:** How a meeting or negotiation ends shapes how both sides remember it. End on a warm note, even after hard bargaining.

### Psychology | Choice overload
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** The idea that too many options make people less likely to choose anything.
**Explained:** In a famous 2000 study, a supermarket stall with 24 jams drew more shoppers than one with 6, but only 3% bought, against 30% at the smaller stall. Later studies were mixed: a 2010 review of 50 experiments found an average effect near zero, though it can appear when choices are complex.
**Example:** Some companies cut their product ranges and sold more; others didn't. Streaming services show "Top 10" rows so you don't face thousands of titles at once.
**Why it matters:** Facing too many options, narrow them with two or three criteria first. And notice when a seller removes options to steer you.

### Psychology | Decoy effect
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Adding a clearly worse option can make one of the original options look better.
**Explained:** Joel Huber and colleagues showed it in 1982. If a decoy is similar to one option but slightly worse, that option starts to look like the obvious winner. It appears reliably in lab studies with simple descriptions, but is weaker and less consistent with real products and pictures.
**Example:** A cinema sells small popcorn for $4 and large for $8. Adding a medium at $7.50 suddenly makes the large look like great value.
**Why it matters:** When one option on a menu is clearly worse than its neighbour, ask why it's there. It might exist to make you choose the neighbour.

### Persuasion | Narrative transportation
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** When we're absorbed in a story, we argue with it less, and its message sinks in more.
**Explained:** Psychologists Melanie Green and Timothy Brock showed in 2000 that the more "transported" readers were into a story, the more their beliefs shifted toward it, and the fewer flaws they spotted. A story doesn't feel like an argument, so our defences stay down.
**Example:** Adverts rarely list facts; they tell a small story, like a father coming home to a dog at the door. Charities tell one person's journey instead of showing statistics.
**Why it matters:** Enjoy the story, then ask afterwards: what was it trying to make me believe, and would I believe it without the story?

### Persuasion | Two-sided arguments
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Admitting the weak points of your case, then answering them, often persuades better than giving only one side.
**Explained:** Research on US Army films in World War II found two-sided messages worked better with educated or doubtful audiences, who would think of the objections anyway. A 1999 review by Daniel O'Keefe found they persuade best when the other side is answered, not just mentioned.
**Example:** "Yes, this laptop is heavier than the others, because of the bigger battery, which lasts twice as long." Admitting the weight makes the battery claim more believable.
**Why it matters:** When someone admits a small flaw, it builds trust, so check they aren't hiding a bigger one. When you persuade, raise the objection before they do.

### Negotiation | Multiple equivalent offers
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Instead of one offer, present two or three packages that are equally good for you.
**Explained:** Researchers including Victoria Medvec and Adam Galinsky studied these "multiple equivalent simultaneous offers" in the 2000s. Seeing which package the other side prefers reveals their priorities, and having a choice makes them feel less pushed. Studies found better deals, and happier counterparts.
**Example:** A freelancer offers: $3,000 delivered in two weeks, $2,600 in four weeks, or $2,800 with a round of revisions included. The client's pick shows whether they care most about speed, price or revisions.
**Why it matters:** A single offer invites a counter-offer; a choice invites a decision. Make sure every option is one you'd be happy with.

### Negotiation | Nibbling
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Asking for a small extra at the very end, after the main deal is agreed.
**Explained:** Once people have mentally closed a deal, they don't want to reopen it over something small, so they often give in. It's common in sales and a classic tactic in negotiation training. Used too often, it destroys trust.
**Example:** After agreeing the price of a car, the buyer says "You'll throw in the floor mats and a full tank, right?" After accepting a job, the new hire asks for an extra day's holiday.
**Why it matters:** Before agreeing, ask "Is there anything else?" so nothing can be added later. If someone nibbles, it's fine to ask for something in return.

## Day XII

### Psychology | Behavioural priming
**Evidence:** Legend
**In short:** The claim that subtle cues, like reading words about old age, change how we act without our noticing.
**Explained:** A famous 1996 study reported that students who unscrambled words like "wrinkle" and "Florida" walked more slowly afterwards. A 2012 repeat using timing sensors found no effect, except when the researchers expected one. Many similar findings have also failed. Simple word priming, where "doctor" speeds up recognising "nurse", is real.
**Example:** In 2012, Daniel Kahneman, who had praised these studies in his best seller, wrote an open letter calling this research the "poster child" for doubts in psychology, and urged researchers to repeat it.
**Why it matters:** Be sceptical of claims that tiny, unnoticed cues control you. Persuasion mostly works through things you could notice if you paid attention.

### Psychology | Placebo effect
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Feeling better after a treatment with no active ingredient, because you expected it to work.
**Explained:** Placebo effects are real for things we feel, such as pain and nausea. But much of what looks like a placebo effect is just symptoms easing by themselves over time. A 2010 review by Asbjørn Hróbjartsson and Peter Gøtzsche found placebos did little for objective outcomes, like blood tests.
**Example:** In studies, branded painkillers worked slightly better for some people than identical unbranded ones, and an expensive placebo was rated as more effective than a cheap one.
**Why it matters:** Expectations shape experience, which is why packaging and price matter. But "it made me feel better" isn't proof that something works.

### Psychology | Hawthorne effect
**Evidence:** Legend
**In short:** The famous claim that workers became more productive simply because researchers were watching them.
**Explained:** It comes from 1920s studies at the Hawthorne Works factory near Chicago, where output seemed to rise whatever the researchers changed, even the lighting. When economists Steven Levitt and John List re-examined the surviving lighting data in 2011, they found little sign of the dramatic effect.
**Example:** The story still appears in management courses as proof that attention alone boosts performance. Being watched can change behaviour, but far less neatly than the legend says.
**Why it matters:** A tidy story that "everyone knows" can rest on thin data. Ask who checked it, and when.

### Persuasion | Elaboration likelihood model
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** We're persuaded in two ways: by thinking hard about arguments, or by quick cues like fame or confidence.
**Explained:** Richard Petty and John Cacioppo proposed it in the 1980s. When we're motivated and able to think (the "central route"), strong arguments win and the change lasts. When we're rushed, tired or uninterested (the "peripheral route"), cues like a celebrity or a confident voice do the work, and the effect fades faster.
**Example:** Buying a car, you compare reviews and costs. Buying chewing gum, a catchy advert with a famous face is enough.
**Why it matters:** People who want quick decisions keep you on the peripheral route by rushing, entertaining or flattering you. For important choices, slow down and take the central route.

### Persuasion | Fear appeals
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Messages that try to persuade by scaring people, such as graphic health warnings.
**Explained:** Fear can work, but mainly when people also believe they can do something effective about the threat. Kim Witte's research in the 1990s showed that strong fear with no clear action makes people avoid or deny the message instead. Reviews find fear appeals work on average, but results vary a lot.
**Example:** Graphic pictures on cigarette packets come with a helpline number: the fear grabs attention, the number offers a way out.
**Why it matters:** When a message scares you, look for what it wants you to do, and whether that really solves the problem or just benefits the sender.

### Negotiation | Good cop, bad cop
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** One negotiator is tough and threatening, while a partner seems friendly and "on your side".
**Explained:** The contrast makes the friendly one's offer feel like relief, so it's accepted more easily. It's a classic interrogation and sales tactic. A 2000 study by Soo Jin Brodt and Marla Tuchinsky found it can work, but it's easy to spot, and once noticed it tends to backfire.
**Example:** A car salesperson says "I'd love to give you this price, but my manager is tough. Let me see what I can do." The manager you never meet is the bad cop.
**Why it matters:** If you spot it, name it lightly ("Feels like good cop, bad cop") and deal with the terms, not the moods.

### Negotiation | The flinch
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Reacting with visible shock or disappointment to an offer, to signal that it's too much.
**Explained:** It's a common tactic in sales and negotiation courses: a sharp breath, a wince, "That much?!" It plays on contrast and reciprocity, and the other side may lower their price just to ease the discomfort. There's little direct research on it, so treat it as practitioner advice.
**Example:** A buyer looks at a quote, winces and says "Oh. I was expecting something very different." The seller hurries to explain what could be removed to make it cheaper.
**Why it matters:** If someone flinches at your offer, don't rush to cut it. Wait, then ask what they had in mind.

## Day XIII

### Psychology | Pygmalion effect
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** The idea that other people's high expectations make us perform better.
**Explained:** In a 1968 study, Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson told teachers some pupils were about to "bloom" intellectually. The pupils had been picked at random, yet some showed bigger IQ gains a year later. Critics found problems with the testing, and later reviews found expectation effects are real but usually small.
**Example:** A manager who expects a new hire to shine gives them more attention, feedback and chances, which helps them shine. The effect works mostly through how people are treated.
**Why it matters:** Expectations matter because they change behaviour, not by magic. Be wary of anyone who says believing in something is enough to make it happen.

### Psychology | Emotional contagion
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Moods spread from person to person, often without anyone noticing.
**Explained:** Elaine Hatfield and colleagues described it in the 1990s: we automatically mimic other people's faces, voices and postures, and that mimicry feeds back into our own feelings. A calm person can settle a room; an anxious one can unsettle it. It happens online too, though usually weakly.
**Example:** One tense person can make a whole negotiation more defensive. Call centres train staff to smile while speaking, because callers can hear it.
**Why it matters:** Your mood is part of your message. Before a tense conversation, set your own tone: others will tend to match it.

### Psychology | Illusory correlation
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Seeing a link between two things that aren't actually related.
**Explained:** Loren and Jean Chapman showed it in the 1960s: people, including trained psychologists, "saw" links between features of drawings and personality traits that weren't in the data. Unusual events stand out in memory, so when two happen together, we remember it and overestimate how often it happens.
**Example:** Many people believe hospitals get busier at full moon. Studies of hospital records mostly find no such link; the busy full-moon nights are just more memorable.
**Why it matters:** Before believing "every time X, Y happens", count the times X happened without Y. Many stereotypes and superstitions grow this way.

### Persuasion | Gain vs loss framing
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Stressing what people gain by acting, or lose by not acting, was expected to change persuasion a lot. Usually it doesn't.
**Explained:** Based on prospect theory, researchers expected "Not getting screened could cost you your life" to beat "Getting screened could save your life". But large reviews by Daniel O'Keefe and Jakob Jensen found the differences are usually tiny, with no reliable winner.
**Example:** A campaign can test "You'll save $150 a year by insulating" against "You're losing $150 a year by not insulating". The winner changes from campaign to campaign.
**Why it matters:** The frame matters less than people think; the substance matters more. Still, notice which frame you're given and translate it into the other.

### Persuasion | Sleeper effect
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** A message from an untrustworthy source can become more persuasive over time, as we forget where we heard it.
**Explained:** Carl Hovland's team noticed it in studies of World War II army films. Later research found it hard to produce reliably: it shows up mainly when the warning about the source comes after a strong message. A 2004 review concluded it's real but only under certain conditions.
**Example:** You read a claim on a dodgy website and dismiss it. Weeks later you remember the claim, but not the website, and it seems more believable.
**Why it matters:** Remember the source as well as the fact. "Where did I hear that?" is a good question before you pass something on.

### Negotiation | Silence
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Staying quiet after an offer or question often gets the other side to say more, or give more.
**Explained:** Silence feels uncomfortable, so people rush to fill it by explaining, justifying or improving their offer. A 2022 study by Jared Curhan and colleagues found deliberate pauses helped negotiators shift from fighting to problem-solving and reach better deals for both. Research is still limited, and too much silence can feel hostile.
**Example:** A buyer hears the price and pauses for five seconds without a word. The seller adds: "Of course, we could do something on delivery."
**Why it matters:** After you make an offer, stop talking. After they make one, count to five before replying.

### Negotiation | Splitting the difference
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Meeting halfway between two positions: quick and fair-sounding, but not always fair.
**Explained:** Splitting feels even-handed, which is why people accept it. But the midpoint depends on where each side started, so the side with the more extreme opening does better. It also skips the search for trades that could suit both sides more.
**Example:** You offer $400 for a sofa listed at $600, and the seller says "Let's split the difference: $500." If $600 was inflated, "halfway" rewards the inflation.
**Why it matters:** Before splitting, ask whether both starting points were reasonable, and whether another trade, like timing or extras, could beat the midpoint for both of you.

## Day XIV

### Psychology | Gambler's fallacy
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Believing that after a run of one result, the opposite is "due", when each event is independent.
**Explained:** A coin has no memory: after five heads, the chance of tails is still one in two. We expect even short runs to look balanced, so streaks feel as if they must end. It isn't only gamblers: studies have found traces of it in the decisions of judges, loan officers and baseball umpires.
**Example:** On 18 August 1913, at the Monte Carlo casino, the roulette ball landed on black 26 times in a row. Gamblers lost fortunes betting on red, sure it was due.
**Why it matters:** When someone says "you're due a win" or "prices can't keep falling", ask whether the events are actually connected.

### Psychology | Base rate neglect
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Ignoring how common something is in general, and focusing on vivid details instead.
**Explained:** In Kahneman and Tversky's 1973 studies, people judged whether someone was an engineer from a short personality sketch, and largely ignored whether the group was 30% or 70% engineers. Vivid details drown out dull statistics, even when the statistics matter more.
**Example:** A test is 99% accurate, and you test positive for a disease that 1 in 10,000 people have. Among 10,000 people, about 1 has it, but about 100 healthy people also test positive. Your chance of having it is around 1%.
**Why it matters:** Before reacting to a striking detail, ask how common the thing is in the first place.

### Psychology | Survivorship bias
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Drawing conclusions only from the winners, because the failures are no longer around to see.
**Explained:** We notice successful companies, famous dropouts and old buildings still standing, but not the many that failed, so their lessons go missing. In World War II, statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that bullet holes on returning planes showed where a plane could be hit and survive; the planes that never returned had been hit elsewhere.
**Example:** "They built things better in the old days." Plenty of old buildings were badly built; they just fell down or were knocked down long ago.
**Why it matters:** When someone says "I did X and succeeded", ask how many did X and failed. Success stories are a very biased sample.

### Persuasion | Inoculation
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Exposing people to a weakened form of a persuasion attempt helps them resist the real thing.
**Explained:** Psychologist William McGuire developed the idea in the 1960s, borrowing from vaccines. Warning people that an argument is coming, and showing its trick in advance, builds resistance. Today it's called "prebunking", and games like *Bad News* from Sander van der Linden's team have been shown to help people spot misinformation.
**Example:** In 2022, Google ran short prebunking videos on YouTube in parts of Europe, showing tactics like scapegoating and false choices. Viewers got better at spotting them.
**Why it matters:** This app is a form of inoculation: learning the tricks in advance makes them easier to spot when someone uses them on you.

### Persuasion | Subliminal advertising
**Evidence:** Legend
**In short:** The claim that hidden, flashed messages in films or adverts make people buy things.
**Explained:** In 1957, market researcher James Vicary said flashing "Eat popcorn" and "Drink Coca-Cola" during a film had lifted popcorn sales by 18% and Coke by 58%. It caused a panic, but in 1962 he admitted the study was made up. Lab studies since show hidden cues have tiny, short effects at most.
**Example:** Several countries banned subliminal advertising anyway. Meanwhile, the persuasion that really works is in plain sight: repetition, stories, social proof.
**Why it matters:** Don't worry about hidden messages. Worry about the obvious ones you've stopped noticing.

### Negotiation | Tactical empathy
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Showing that you understand the other side's feelings and view, without necessarily agreeing with them.
**Explained:** Former FBI hostage negotiator Chris Voss popularised the term in *Never Split the Difference* (2016). The idea: people who feel understood get less defensive and more cooperative. Research on "perspective-taking" supports parts of it, but the specific techniques come mainly from experience rather than controlled studies.
**Example:** "It sounds like suppliers have let you down before, so you're wary of committing." Understanding their caution doesn't mean giving in to it.
**Why it matters:** Understanding isn't agreeing. Showing someone you get their position often makes them more open to yours.

### Negotiation | Labelling emotions
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** Naming the other person's emotion out loud: "It seems like you're frustrated by this."
**Explained:** Chris Voss recommends it as a core negotiation tool. It builds on research by Matthew Lieberman and colleagues showing that putting feelings into words calms the brain's alarm response. But that research mostly had people label their own feelings, not someone else's, so the negotiation use is less tested.
**Example:** A customer is angry about a late delivery. "It sounds like this delay has really messed up your week" often calms things faster than explaining why it happened.
**Why it matters:** Name the emotion before arguing the facts. Say "it seems like…", not "you are…", so it's easy for them to correct you.

## Day XV

### Psychology | The 7-38-55 rule
**Evidence:** Legend
**In short:** The claim that communication is 7% words, 38% tone of voice and 55% body language.
**Explained:** The numbers come from two small 1967 studies by Albert Mehrabian. People heard single words, like "dear" or "terrible", said in a tone that matched or clashed with the word, and sometimes saw a face. The numbers only describe judging someone's feelings when words and tone clash. Mehrabian has said they don't apply to communication in general.
**Example:** The rule is still quoted in presentation courses. But if 93% of meaning were nonverbal, you could follow a lecture in a language you don't speak.
**Why it matters:** Tone and body language matter, especially when they clash with the words. But the words still carry most of the meaning.

### Psychology | Learning styles
**Evidence:** Legend
**In short:** The popular idea that people learn best when teaching matches their "style": visual, auditory or hands-on.
**Explained:** People do have preferences. But a 2008 review by Harold Pashler and colleagues found almost no good evidence that matching teaching to someone's preferred style improves learning. What helps is matching the method to the material: diagrams for maps, sound for music, practice for skills.
**Example:** A 2012 survey found over 90% of teachers in the UK and the Netherlands believed in learning styles, and many schools have tested pupils to find theirs.
**Why it matters:** "I'm a visual learner" can become an excuse. Testing yourself and spacing out practice help everyone, which is why this app has Recall.

### Psychology | The 10% brain myth
**Evidence:** Legend
**In short:** The claim that we only use 10% of our brains.
**Explained:** Brain scans show activity all over the brain across a day, even during sleep, and damage to almost any area causes problems. The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of our energy; evolution wouldn't keep such an expensive organ mostly idle. The myth may come from misquoted early psychologists talking about untapped potential.
**Example:** Films like *Lucy* (2014) and self-help products promise to unlock the "other 90%". There's nothing to unlock: you're already using it.
**Why it matters:** When a product promises to unlock hidden potential, ask for the evidence. Myths that flatter us are the easiest to believe.

### Persuasion | The yes ladder
**Evidence:** Debated
**In short:** A sales tactic: get someone saying yes to easy questions, so they keep saying yes to the one that counts.
**Explained:** It's related to foot-in-the-door and commitment, which have research behind them. But the idea that a string of easy yeses builds a "yes habit" comes mostly from sales training, with little direct evidence. Obvious yes-questions can also feel manipulative and put people on guard.
**Example:** "You want your family to be safe, right? And safety is worth investing in? So you'd want the best alarm system?"
**Why it matters:** When a conversation is full of questions you can only answer yes to, notice where it's heading. You can agree with the first two and still say no to the third.

### Persuasion | Rule of three
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** Ideas grouped in threes are more memorable and persuasive than twos or fours.
**Explained:** Classical rhetoric called a list of three a tricolon. Three is the smallest number that makes a pattern: enough to feel complete, short enough to remember. A 2014 study found three positive claims about a product persuaded best; a fourth made people more sceptical.
**Example:** "Veni, vidi, vici" (Julius Caesar). "Government of the people, by the people, for the people" (Abraham Lincoln). "Stop, look and listen."
**Why it matters:** Use three points when you want to be remembered. When a message sounds unusually neat, check the substance, not just the rhythm.

### Negotiation | Winner's curse
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** The winner of an auction often overpays, because winning means you valued the prize more than anyone else did.
**Explained:** When many bidders estimate an uncertain value, the highest estimate is likely to be too high. Engineers at the oil company Atlantic Richfield described it in 1971, after noticing that companies winning bids for offshore oil fields often lost money on them. It also shows up in company takeovers and house bidding wars.
**Example:** In classroom auctions for a jar of coins, the average bid is often below the jar's value, but the winning bid is usually above it.
**Why it matters:** If your offer is accepted instantly, ask whether you overpaid. In a bidding war, set your limit before the excitement starts.

### Negotiation | Ultimatum game
**Evidence:** Solid
**In short:** An experiment showing people will give up money to punish an offer they see as unfair.
**Explained:** In the game, first studied by Werner Güth and colleagues in 1982, one player proposes how to split a sum of money; the other can accept, or reject so both get nothing. Pure self-interest says accept anything. In practice, offers below about a quarter are often rejected, and most people offer 40–50%.
**Example:** Offered $2 out of $10, many people would rather walk away with nothing than accept what feels like an insult.
**Why it matters:** Fairness matters, not just value. A deal that feels insulting can be rejected even when it would leave the other side better off.
